### How a soldier from the ranks came to fly

**Observers and gunners were not the same thing.** Most observers in two-seaters were officers;
infantry officers were gazetted to the RFC's General List as "Flying Officer (Observer)". A man
from the ranks usually flew as an **aerial gunner** (or gunlayer), and could later be remustered
as an observer. Still, observers "could be any rank", and it was "far from uncommon" for air
mechanics 2nd class (a private's equivalent) to fly on operations.

**The usual road was through the ground crew.** Most gunners from the ranks were already in the
RFC as air mechanics and volunteered to fly. Infantrymen did come across: an Army Cyclist Corps
man was "Aerial Gunner (Probationary)" in June 1917; a West Kent private became a gunner, then an
observer; and the RFC had "a lengthy waiting list of applicants from both the infantry and
civilians".

**What is not found** is the paperwork by which a private in a battalion applied. Assume an
application through his CO, a posting to a squadron as a probationary gunner, and flying on
operations before he had qualified.

**Rank.** RFC other ranks were air mechanics (3rd, 2nd or 1st class), then corporal and sergeant,
with "Aerial Gunner" as a qualification. The examples rose fast: Emsden, an FE2b gunner, went
from AM2 to corporal to sergeant; Deighton, a corporal who volunteered as a gunner, was a sergeant
by his DCM. When the RAF formed on 1 April 1918, the RFC ranks became private and sergeant.

**Training.** No 1 School of Aerial Gunnery at Hythe, set up in 1916 as a detached part of the
School of Musketry, was the only gunnery school until 1917. Its first job was training observers,
"or 'aerial gunners' as they became known"; some men met the Lewis gun there for the first time.
By 1918 observers generally passed a course before reaching a squadron.

**A Lewis gunner is the natural man.** The gunner's weapon is a Lewis gun on a ring mount. A
battalion Lewis gunner already knows its stoppages, which is the skill that matters at altitude
with frozen hands.

### The work

**The aeroplanes.** The FE2b, a pusher with the gunner standing in the nose, obsolete by 1917.
The RE8, the standard artillery-spotting machine from mid-1917 to the end. The Bristol Fighter,
two per corps squadron from early 1918. The DH4 day bomber, 1917-18.

**The jobs.** Artillery observation: one-way wireless in Morse from the aeroplane to the
battery, which answered with ground signals. Photography. Line and offensive patrols, and
bombing. **Contact patrol:** below 1,000 feet over an attack, the aeroplane sounds its Klaxon
(Morse "A": where are you?), the infantry light flares, and the observer marks the line on his
map. This is how a gunner sees his own battalion.

**Parachutes: none for aeroplane crews.** Balloon observers had static-line Calthrop parachutes,
and Calthrop's "Guardian Angel" for aeroplanes existed before the war. The RFC's commander would
not issue it: officially, not fully safe, too bulky, too heavy. It was adopted as the war ended.
**Not proven:** the popular explanation, that parachutes would make men abandon their machines.
One source says there is no evidence for it, so no officer should say it in the game as fact.

### The cost

**Bloody April 1917, over Arras.** At least 245 aircraft lost. Aircrew losses vary by source:
"211 killed or missing and 108 prisoners", "316 killed, missing or taken", "over 400 killed or
missing".

One figure appears in a published history (Peter Hart): a new subaltern's life expectancy at the
front fell to **eleven days**. The popular "17½ flying hours" figure appears only in an
AI-generated encyclopedia: do not use it.

**Amiens, 8 August 1918.** The RAF tried to break the Somme bridges and lost heavily: one count
is 44 aircraft lost and 52 badly damaged; low-flying aeroplanes ran at 23% wastage that day.

**The whole war.** RFC/RAF totals vary: about 6,166 killed, 7,245 wounded and 3,212 missing in one
count, and 8,136 dead in Canada's official history. These include training accidents in Britain.
No rate for gunners alone was found.

**The shape for the war layer.** Almost no shellfire at the aerodrome; a real chance of death on
every flight; a man brought down on the German side was usually killed or captured.

**Pay.** RFC flying pay for other ranks is confirmed only for NCO pilots: 4s a day first class,
2s second class (1913 and 1916). **No rate for aerial gunners was found.** Treat it as tuned.

## The design

### Who is offered it, and when

- **When.** Once, on the tour page of May 1917 (tour 24, stage 3), after Bloody April. The squadrons
  over Arras need gunners; an officer comes round asking for Lewis gunners who will fly.
- **Who.** Any town: Lewis gun 2 or more (the natural route; most often a mill man, since
  machinery speeds the Lewis course); in the line that tour; not transferred, not commissioned,
  sergeant or below.
- **One offer**, the first in the middle of a stage (the tunneller's comes at a stage start; a
  `pending` asked at a tour start already allows it). A man who took the tanks is already gone.

### What he gives up

The same as the tunneller: he leaves the section for good, his friends fight on in every battle
and can die (`finishWar`), and the grade counts who comes home.

**New: he sees them from above.** In each battle he flies contact patrol over the battalion.
Choosing `push` ("stay down low and find their flares") brings the guns onto what holds them up,
and cuts the friends' loss chance that phase by ×0.85. He is never `present`.

### What changes in the war layer

A new path, `air`. Proposed numbers, set beside the existing paths, for the gate to tune:

| | Rifleman | Tunneller | Air gunner (proposed) | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Line shellfire per tour (`fire`) | 1.0 | 0.9 | 0.1 | The aerodrome is miles back; night bombing only |
| Nerve drain per tour | 1.0 | 1.0 | 0.5 | Beds, a mess, a bath. The strain comes from flying |
| Role mission | specialists, 60% | 60% | 85%, hit 0.08, nerve 7 | Flying every week is the job |
| Battle phases | the battalion's | the battalion's | a new phase type `air` | Below |
| Award points | 1.0 | 0.9 | 1.4 | Gunners from the ranks won DCMs, MMs and later DFMs |
| Killed share of a hit | 0.32 | 0.32 | 0.45 | No parachute |
| Captured share of a hit | 0 | 0 | 0.15 | Down on the wrong side of the line |
| Duties | 60% of tours | none | none | |
| Stands in at rest | the section | crew of 4 | 2 (his pilot and the machine's fitter), plus `restBonus` +2 | A pair, not a section; better billets |

**Battles.** On the battalion's battle days he flies over the same battle: Passchendaele in
1917, then the Spring Offensive, Amiens and October in 1918. Arras is over before he joins. Each
phase becomes the new type `air`, with its own `PHASE_HIT` row (proposed): `push` "stay on the
job" (the shoot, the photographs, the low pass) ×1.2; `help` "watch the pilot's tail" ×0.9;
`ground` "tell him to turn for home" ×0.6, costing standing as now.

Freezing under fire still exists: at low nerve the gun is not fired.

**The Spring Offensive.** It is fought as low-level strafing of the advancing columns. Its
`capture` chance applies to him only through the wound table, not through being overrun.

**Role missions** (`ROLES.air`, sortie key `air`). Each is three steps; the sortie skill is
`lewis`, and machinery gives the aptitude. Step 2 is the gun or the work, and turning back is
allowed at step 2 ("the engine's missing; we go home"). The kinds, varied by the text: an
artillery shoot (RE8, the wireless, the battery's strips), a photographic run, a line patrol,
and in 1918 a bombing raid.

**Home Establishment.** A rested man going home was a normal event in the RFC. Two ways in: at
the 1917 leave, an instructor's posting (Hythe) offered with chance 0.7 in `postingOffers`; and,
when his nerve is under 25, the `sick` question offering "the MO sends you home", which succeeds
half the time. Both end the run as a posting, graded ×1.0. The trade: the riskiest flights in the
game, but the likeliest earned road home.

**Pay** (`PAY.airPence`). Rank pay plus 2s (24d) a day of flying pay. This is tuned, not sourced:
2s is the confirmed rate for an NCO pilot second class, not for a gunner.

**Rank and promotion.** He keeps his number of steps; the Soldier tab shows the RFC title (AM2
for a private) until 1 April 1918, then the RAF's. After his first two successful missions he
qualifies as an aerial gunner and is made sergeant if below it, as Emsden and Deighton were: the
"slightly better circumstances" of the author's rule for roles. No pilot's path: pilot training
is offered at the 1917 leave as a home posting (open question 2).

**Awards.** MM and DCM as now. From June 1918 the DFM replaces the MM for him: it needs a new award
key and grade weight, equal to the MM's 8.

## The story

### The pilot and the pair

| | Who | Thread |
|---|---|---|
| Lt Neil Ferrier (pilot, 1917) | A Manitoba farm boy, twenty; flies the RE8 "like a binder" | Talks to him across the class line the squadron keeps. Worn out by winter: Home Establishment in February 1918, or killed in March, by the dice |
| Sgt Len Hollis (pilot, 1918) | A fitter from the ranks who learned to fly; a sergeant pilot | Knows engines and says so. With him at Amiens and the bridges |
| AM1 Bert Sallis (fitter) | Older, a Coventry cycle works man; looks after the machine | Waits on the field for them every evening, counting |

`ROLE.crew` for air is 2: the pilot and Sallis. Each has a death or loss scene. The pilot is lost
by the same kind of roll that takes the section's friends, on the flights the gunner survives:
the machine comes down with a dead man at the controls, the gunner lives through it (a wound in
the war layer), and he flies with the next pilot.

### 1917 (from tour 24, May, to tour 29)

- May: the officer asks for Lewis gunners who will fly; the battalion's opinion of it.
- May: the aerodrome; the smell of castor oil and dope; a bed with sheets.
- June: his first shoot; the clock code; the battery's white strips on the grass.
- June: the gun jams at 6,000 feet with frozen hands; what the battalion's Lewis course taught him.
- July: the photographs: the battalion's own trenches from above, "like a scar".
- August: Passchendaele from above: the Klaxon, and the battalion's flares, or no flares.
- September: Archie; a machine burning on the way down, and no parachute.
- October: Ferrier's letters from Manitoba; the class line in the messes.
- Both roads: his mother's letter. She has read about the airmen in the papers and is more
  frightened than before.

### 1918 (tours 30-39)

- January: Hythe, or the squadron's own gunnery; the winged badge, if he qualifies.
- February: Ferrier goes home, or does not; Hollis arrives.
- March: strafing the columns on 21 March and after; the aerodrome moved back three times.
- April: the RAF forms on 1 April. Nobody has the new uniform, and nothing changes.
- May: a German two-seater's gunner, seen close: a man doing his job.
- June: the DFM, if it comes; the sergeants' mess.
- August: Amiens; the Somme bridges at fifty feet.
- September: Sallis counts the machines home, and one is missing.
- October: the last patrols; the roads full of Germans going back.

### Leaves and the end

- **The 1917 leave.** The town thinks airmen are knights. His sisters want to know about
  Richthofen; his father wants to know about the pay. The instructor's posting, and pilot
  training, are offered here.
- **The 1918 Armistice.** At the aerodrome: Very lights fired into the morning, Hollis and Sallis.
  Then each town's own sound: the mill's buzzer, the pit's buzzer, the farm's bells, the City's
  paper.
- **Word from the battalion.** The battalion's men are seen, not heard: flares in a shell-hole
  line, a column on a road. News comes in letters from home and from the battery's wireless
  operator, a battalion man's cousin. The tour-page line "Word comes over from the battalion..."
  gains an air take ("at the aerodrome, from a battery runner").

## Measurement

**The gate's targets** (SPEC, 2026-11-08) must still hold: best play reaches the Armistice in
5-10% of runs, reckless near 0%, competent play survives 1915 in 60-75%. The offer is in May
1917, so the 1915 and 1916 rows must not move.

**A new gate table, "The air transfer"** (eligible men, 1,000 runs, ± 1 SE), in the tunneller's
form. Columns: takes it vs refuses it, for Armistice, killed, captured, posting, home alive,
friends home, and grade.
- **The pass:** a real trade. Neither road is best on both survival and grade.
- **The shape wanted:** killed and captured above the infantry's; more postings home; more
  awards; an Armistice rate no higher than refusing.
- **The posting check.** If Home Establishment makes taking the air road the best way home, the
  ×0.7 offer chance comes down. That is the one number most likely to need tuning.

**Story share.** `npx tsx tools/story-share.ts 200` on each road, near half story in 1917 (May
onward) and 1918 (target 45-60%). Missions on 85% of tours are there to stop the tunneller's 70%.

**The usual checks.** Transcripts of a full air road read end to end; the validator, style lint,
continuity (the pilot and fitter guarded like friends) and the render scan.

## Open questions for the author

1. **When is it offered?** Recommended: May 1917, after Bloody April, when the squadrons needed
   men. The alternative is January 1918, which is shorter and safer to build but misses
   Passchendaele from above.
2. **Can he train as a pilot?** Recommended: no flying path. Pilot training is offered at the
   1917 leave as a home posting: months in England, graded like an instructor's post. A pilot
   path would be a third transfer's worth of content.
3. **Home Establishment as an exit?** Recommended: yes. Offered at the leave, and through the MO
   when he is worn out. It was the RFC's real habit, and it balances the highest risk per tour in
   the game. The gate decides the chance.
4. **Ranks on the Soldier tab?** Recommended: show the RFC or RAF title (AM2, Sergeant), with the
   army equivalent beside it. He would have been called by the new title, and the player needs
   the old one to read the ladder.
